# Two-hundred years ago, Jean-François Champollion deciphers the Rosetta Stone (JSTOR Daily)

## Source Snapshot

*Verbatim excerpts from https://daily.jstor.org/jean-francois-champollion-deciphers-the-rosetta-stone/ (captured 2026-08-10). Copyright JSTOR Daily; internal benchmark use only.*

> Two-hundred years ago, French scholar and polymath Jean-François Champollion announced he had deciphered the Rosetta Stone. His September 27, 1822, presentation to the Académie des Inscriptions et Belles-Lettres in Paris—where the audience included his main rival, English polymath Thomas Young—would radically change the study of ancient Egypt.

> When the Rosetta Stone was inscribed, it wasn't a singular object; rather it was one of many of its type. Its text praising the divine virtues of the pharaoh Ptolemy V (crowned in 196 BCE) was spread across Egypt through the erection of similar stelae.

> The Rosetta Stone was a form of propaganda, then. The inscription, written in three languages—formal hieroglyphs, demotic (the "everyday" Egyptian script), and ancient Greek—declared that its readers must hail Ptolemy V, "the god who maketh himself manifest, whose deeds are beautiful."

> By the time of Napoleon Bonaparte's campaign in Egypt in 1798, hieroglyphs were impenetrable, with researchers unsure if they operated like pictograms or phonetic symbols. The exact story of the Rosetta Stone's rediscovery is a little ambiguous, but it's largely held that on July 15, 1799, French soldiers excavating for work on a fort near the port city of Rosetta (today's Rashid) on the Nile delta found the fragmented slab.

> The stone was only briefly in French hands; as a spoil of war, it was transferred to the British after the defeat of Napoleon and the signing of the Treaty of Alexandria in 1801.
