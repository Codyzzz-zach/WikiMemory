# Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science

## Source Snapshot

*Note: Full text behind paywall. Content sourced from publicly available abstract, ResearchGate preprint, and OSF project page (https://osf.io/ezcuj/).*

**Authors:** Open Science Collaboration (270+ contributors)
**Journal:** Science, Vol. 349, Issue 6251, aac4716
**Published:** 28 August 2015
**DOI:** 10.1126/science.aac4716

### Abstract (verbatim from ResearchGate/public sources)

"Reproducibility is a defining feature of science, but the extent to which it characterizes current research is unknown. We conducted replications of 100 experimental and correlational studies published in three psychology journals using high-powered designs and original materials when available."

### Key Results (verbatim from abstract)

"Replication effects (Mr = .197, SD = .257) were half the magnitude of original effects (Mr = .403, SD = .188), representing a substantial decline."

"Ninety-seven percent of original studies had significant results (p < .05). Thirty-six percent of replications had significant results; 47% of original effect sizes were in the 95% confidence interval of the replication effect size; 39% of effects were subjectively rated to have replicated the original result; and, if no bias in original results is assumed, combining original and replication results left 68% with significant effects."

### Conclusion (verbatim from abstract)

"Correlational tests suggest that replication success was better predicted by the strength of original evidence than by characteristics of the original and replication teams."

### Additional detail from OSF project page

The Reproducibility Project: Psychology began in November 2011, finished primary data collection in December 2014, and published a summary of the results in August 2015. The project was coordinated by the Center for Open Science. Ultimately over 270 contributors earned authorship on the summary report and 86 others provided volunteer support.

## Research Notes

- This is the landmark paper that catalyzed the "replication crisis" discourse in psychology.
- Source role P-primary: the original large-scale replication study itself.
- Key metrics have been disputed by Gilbert et al. (2016) — see c-psych-003.
- The paper's sampling frame (3 journals, 2008) is a known limitation.
- All replication materials, data, and protocols are open-access on OSF.
- The 39% subjective replication rate vs 36% significance rate highlights the multi-dimensional nature of replication assessment.
